Both ETFs and mutual funds let you buy a diversified basket of stocks or bonds in a single purchase instead of picking individual companies. The question isn't really "which is better" in the abstract — it's which structure fits how and where you're investing.

Here's the actual difference, and how to decide between them for your situation.

## What They Have in Common

Before the differences, it's worth being clear on what's the same: both ETFs (exchange-traded funds) and mutual funds pool money from many investors and use it to buy a collection of underlying assets — stocks, bonds, or a mix. Buying one share gets you instant diversification across dozens or hundreds of holdings, which is the whole reason both exist as an alternative to picking individual stocks.

Both come in passive versions (tracking an index like the S&P 500) and active versions (a manager picking holdings to try to beat the market). If you're new to the passive approach generally, [our guide to index funds for beginners](/investing/index-funds-for-beginners/) covers why low-cost, broad-market index investing is the standard recommendation most financial advisors give.

## The Core Differences

| Feature | ETF | Mutual Fund |
|---|---|---|
| **Trading** | Buy/sell any time market is open, price changes throughout the day | Trades once per day, priced after market close |
| **Minimum investment** | Often $0-$1 (fractional shares) | Sometimes $0, sometimes $1,000-$3,000 |
| **Typical fees** | Usually low, especially for index ETFs | Ranges from low (index funds) to high (actively managed) |
| **Tax efficiency (taxable accounts)** | Generally more tax-efficient | Can trigger more capital gains distributions |
| **Where to buy** | Any standard brokerage account | Sometimes fund-company-specific or brokerage-restricted |

The trading mechanism is the most fundamental difference. An ETF trades on an exchange exactly like a stock — you can buy at 10:15am and sell at 2:30pm the same day at whatever price the market offers in that moment. A mutual fund only transacts once per day: every buy and sell order placed during the day gets executed at a single price calculated after the market closes (called the net asset value, or NAV).

For most long-term investors, this distinction matters less than it sounds. If you're buying and holding for years, intraday price movement is close to irrelevant.

## Fees: Where the Real Money Is Won or Lost

Fees are the difference most likely to actually affect your returns over time, and they have nothing to do with ETF vs. mutual fund as a structure — they come down to passive vs. active management.

- **Passive index ETFs and index mutual funds**: often 0.02%-0.10% expense ratio, and the gap between the two structures tracking the same index is usually tiny.
- **Actively managed mutual funds**: commonly 0.5%-1.5% or higher, since you're paying for a manager and research team trying to beat the market.
- **Actively managed ETFs**: exist and have grown more common, but a plain index ETF is still typically the lowest-cost option available.

A 1% annual fee difference sounds small but compounds significantly. On a $10,000 investment growing for 30 years, the difference between a 0.05% fee and a 1% fee can amount to tens of thousands of dollars in lost growth — money paid to the fund company instead of staying invested and compounding for you.

## Taxes: The ETF Structural Advantage

In a taxable brokerage account (not a 401(k) or IRA), ETFs generally come out ahead for a structural reason: the way ETF shares are created and redeemed lets most of them avoid distributing taxable capital gains to shareholders most years, even when the fund sells holdings internally.

Mutual funds, especially actively managed ones with frequent trading, are more likely to distribute capital gains to all shareholders at year-end — meaning you could owe taxes on a fund's gains even if you didn't sell a single share yourself.

This difference disappears entirely inside a 401(k), traditional IRA, or Roth IRA, since those accounts are already tax-advantaged and don't generate a current-year tax bill from fund distributions either way.

## Which One Should You Actually Pick?

**Pick an ETF if:**
- You're investing through a standard taxable brokerage account
- You want the flexibility to buy at a specific price point during the day
- You're starting with a small amount and want to avoid mutual fund minimums

**Pick a mutual fund if:**
- Your 401(k) plan only offers mutual funds (common — check your plan menu)
- You want to set up automatic recurring investments in a fixed dollar amount without worrying about fractional share support
- You're already invested in a specific low-cost index mutual fund (like many Vanguard or Fidelity options) and switching would trigger unnecessary transaction friction

In practice, for a retirement account like a 401(k), the choice is often made for you — you pick from whatever menu your employer's plan offers, which [is worth understanding fully](/investing/what-is-a-401k/) before you select where new contributions go. For an IRA or taxable account you open yourself, a low-cost index ETF tracking a broad market index (like a total U.S. stock market fund) is the more common default recommendation today.

## Getting Started

If you don't have a brokerage account yet, opening one takes about 10 minutes at any major provider, and most now support both ETFs and mutual funds with no minimum. Once it's open, start with a single broad-market index ETF or mutual fund rather than trying to build a complex multi-fund portfolio right away — you can always add more specific holdings later once the basics are in place.

Make sure your day-to-day finances are stable before committing new money to either one: if you're still building your safety net, [get your emergency fund in place first](/investing/emergency-fund-before-investing/) so a short-term cash need never forces you to sell investments at a bad time.
