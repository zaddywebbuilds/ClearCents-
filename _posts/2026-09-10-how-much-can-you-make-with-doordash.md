# How Much Can You Really Make With DoorDash? The Net-Pay Breakdown

DoorDash marketing shows the number before costs. The honest question is what lands in your bank account after gas, car wear, and taxes — and that figure is a lot lower than the dashboard total. Here is the full math.

## What DoorDash actually pays

Every offer is built from three parts:

- **Base pay:** $2 to $10+ per delivery, set by distance, time, and how undesirable the order is.
- **Tips:** you keep 100%. Tips are often 40 to 60 percent of total earnings, so customer service and speed matter.
- **Promotions:** Peak Pay (a per-order bonus during busy windows) and Challenges (a bonus for completing X deliveries in a set period).

Across all Dashers, **gross pay runs about $12 to $16 per active hour**. Drivers who decline low offers and only run high-dollar deliveries push that to **$20 to $25 per active hour**. But "active hour" only counts time you are on a delivery — the unpaid minutes waiting for the next order pull your real rate down another 15 to 30 percent.

## The expenses nobody advertises

You are a 1099 contractor. Every cost is yours:

| Cost | Rough amount | On a 100-mile shift |
|---|---|---|
| Gas | ~10% of earnings, or $0.10–0.15/mile | $10–15 |
| Maintenance, tires, depreciation | $0.15–0.25/mile | $15–25 |
| Phone, insurance rider, hot bag | $10–30/month | ~$1–2/shift |
| Self-employment tax | 15.3% of net profit | varies |

Vehicle cost alone is close to **$1 per mile** once you count the car wearing out faster. A $3 offer that requires a 5-mile round trip is a money loser after you subtract $5 in true vehicle cost.

## Real net pay: a worked example

You Dash 20 hours over a week, mostly dinner rushes and Saturday:

- Gross pay: 20 hours x $22/active-hour x 0.85 (waiting time) = **~$374**
- Miles driven: ~180
- Vehicle cost: 180 x $0.35 (gas + wear, conservative) = **-$63**
- Set aside for taxes: 25% of profit (~$311) = **-$78**
- **Net take-home: ~$233, or about $11.65 per hour worked**

Selective, fast, in a dense zone you might net $15 to $18 an hour. New, accepting everything, in a spread-out suburb you might net $8 to $10.

Expect your first two or three weeks to be on the low end. Earnings ramp as you learn which restaurants are slow, which neighborhoods tip, and when the rushes actually hit in your market. Scheduling a Dash block in advance also gets you priority over drivers using "Dash Now," which matters in busy zones where slots fill up.

## How to raise your hourly rate

1. **Reject low $/mile offers.** Aim for at least $1.50 to $2.00 per mile. Decline the rest without guilt.
2. **Work the peaks.** Lunch (11am–1pm), dinner (5pm–8pm), Friday and Saturday nights, and bad-weather days carry the highest base pay, tips, and Peak Pay.
3. **Stay in a tight, dense area.** Short deliveries in a small radius mean more orders per hour and less gas burned.
4. **Take stacked orders** when they are on the same route — two deliveries, one drive.
5. **Multi-app.** Run DoorDash alongside another delivery platform and take whichever offer is better in the moment.

## Taxes: don't get surprised in April

You will get a 1099-NEC if you earn $600 or more. Track every mile you drive for deliveries — the IRS standard mileage deduction (70 cents per mile for 2025; check the current year's rate) usually beats deducting actual costs and can wipe out most of your taxable profit. Use a mileage-tracking app, and move 25 to 30 percent of each payout into a separate account for federal and self-employment tax.

## Is it worth it?

For a side hustle, DoorDash is one of the fastest ways to turn spare hours into cash: no interview, no schedule, daily payouts, quit anytime. It is excellent for a short-term goal — the extra $200 to $400 a week can [build a $1,000 starter fund](/save-money/how-to-save-1000-dollars-in-3-months/) in a month or two. It is a poor long-term plan, because net pay often lands near minimum wage and there are no benefits or raises.

## The bottom line

DoorDash grosses most Dashers $12 to $16 per active hour and nets $10 to $18 after gas, car wear, and taxes. Selective drivers working dinner and weekend peaks in dense areas do meaningfully better; drivers who accept every low offer in the suburbs barely clear minimum wage. Treat it as a business — track miles, reject bad offers, set aside tax money — and use it to hit a specific savings target rather than as a permanent paycheck.
