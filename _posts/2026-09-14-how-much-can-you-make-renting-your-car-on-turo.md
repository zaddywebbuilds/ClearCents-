# How Much Can You Make Renting Your Car on Turo? (Real Numbers)

Turo lets you rent out a car you already own to travelers who'd otherwise book a rental counter — and for a vehicle that mostly sits in a driveway, it can turn into genuine monthly income. But the headline earnings numbers you see in ads rarely account for Turo's commission or what the extra mileage does to your car. Here's the real math.

## What the average host actually earns

Earnings data from Turo hosts and independent tracking puts average income at:

- **$634 to $874 per month, per car** (figures vary by source and region)
- **$10,000-$10,500 per year** for an actively-listed vehicle
- **Top-performing hosts:** $1,500-$2,000/month for one well-priced car in a high-demand market

Hosts who list three or more vehicles average closer to **$2,330/month combined** — but that comes with proportionally more cleaning, coordination, and wear across a small fleet, not passive income at scale.

## What actually drives earnings up or down

**Location is the single biggest factor.** Cars listed near major airports, in dense metro areas, or in cities with weak rental-car supply (a recurring issue at several U.S. airports) book far more consistently than the same car in a suburban market.

**Vehicle type matters more than you'd expect.** Practical, fuel-efficient sedans and SUVs book steadily for everyday trips. Specialty or luxury vehicles can command a much higher day rate, but book less often and carry higher repair costs if something goes wrong.

**Availability compounds.** A car listed as available 25+ days a month earns dramatically more than one listed sporadically — Turo's own search ranking also favors hosts with high availability and fast response times, so partial listings get less visibility, not just fewer bookings.

## The fee structure nobody puts in the headline

Turo doesn't take a flat cut — you choose a protection plan, and it determines your split:

| Protection plan | Your payout | Turo's cut | Your liability |
|---|---|---|---|
| Basic | ~60% | ~40% | Lowest (Turo covers most damage) |
| Standard | ~75-80% | ~20-25% | Moderate |
| Premium/Minimal | ~90% | ~10% | Highest (you cover most damage) |

Most first-time hosts choose Standard — a reasonable payout with meaningful protection while they learn how the platform handles claims.

## A realistic net-earnings example

Say your car books 15 days in a month at $65/day on the Standard plan:

- **Gross trip revenue:** 15 × $65 = **$975**
- **Turo's cut (22%):** **−$215**
- **Payout to you:** **$760**
- **Cleaning between trips (15 days, ~$15 each):** **−$225** (or your own time if you DIY)
- **Extra wear/maintenance reserve (~$0.08/mile, ~40 mi/day avg):** **−$180**
- **Net profit: ~$355/month**, or about $24 per booked day

A less-active month — 8 bookings instead of 15 — nets closer to **$150-$190** once the same per-trip costs are subtracted. The gap between gross payout and real profit is exactly why treating Turo income as pure "found money" undercounts the actual cost of running a car more.

## How to get started

1. **Check your personal auto policy first.** Some insurers exclude coverage the moment a car is listed on a rental platform, even before it's booked — confirm this before your first listing goes live.
2. **List with real photos and a specific, honest description.** Listings with 15+ photos and clear mileage/pickup instructions get booked more and generate fewer disputes.
3. **Price competitively for your first month.** Undercutting similar local listings by 10-15% builds reviews quickly; reviews are the biggest lever on future booking rate.
4. **Set your availability wide, then narrow it.** Start with more available days than you think you want — you can always block dates off, but a thin calendar gets deprioritized in search.
5. **Track mileage and expenses from day one.** Turo income is 1099 self-employment income; the IRS standard mileage or actual-expense deduction can offset a meaningful share of what you owe.

## Is it worth it?

For a second car, a car you'd otherwise let sit, or a vehicle you're planning to sell in a year or two anyway, Turo is a legitimate way to add $200-$800 a month with minimal daily effort once it's listed. It's a strong complement to other [gig-economy side hustles](/side-hustles/how-much-can-you-make-driving-for-uber-or-lyft/) if you'd rather monetize an asset than your own hours.

For a primary vehicle you rely on daily, the math is tighter — every day it's rented is a day you don't have it, and the added wear shortens its useful life. Treat any Turo income the way you would [any other side income](/budgeting/zero-based-budgeting-guide/): give it a job in your budget, whether that's [building your first $1,000 emergency fund](/save-money/how-to-save-1000-dollars-in-3-months/) or chipping away at a [credit card balance](/debt-free/how-to-pay-off-credit-card-debt/), instead of letting it blend into regular spending.

## The bottom line

Turo hosts average $634-$874 a month per car before real profit lands closer to $350-$500 once Turo's commission, cleaning, and added wear are subtracted. Location, vehicle type, and availability are the three biggest levers. Treat it as a way to monetize a car that's already sitting idle — not a guaranteed passive-income number pulled straight from the payout screen.
