# How to Save Money on Prescription Drugs (Without Skipping Doses)

Skipping doses or splitting pills to make a prescription last longer is one of the most common — and quietly dangerous — things people do to manage healthcare costs. The good news is that prescription costs have more legitimate levers than almost any other household expense, and most people haven't pulled any of them. Here's how to actually lower what you pay.

## Check GoodRx Before You Fill Anything

GoodRx and similar discount platforms (RxSaver, NeedyMeds) aggregate negotiated group rates at pharmacies and show you the lowest price nearby for any specific drug and dose. It's free to use and frequently beats insurance prices — sometimes by a lot. On common generics, the GoodRx price at a warehouse club pharmacy can be $4-$10 for a 90-day supply. That beats most insurance copays without using your benefits at all.

The process: look up your medication on GoodRx, pick the best local price, show the pharmacist the coupon code or app screen when you drop off the prescription. That's it. The pharmacist will typically process it alongside or instead of your insurance, whichever is lower.

## Ask for the Generic by Name

Most brand-name drugs have a generic equivalent — the same active ingredient, the same dose, the same FDA bioequivalence standards, often 80-90% cheaper. The issue is that pharmacies and doctors don't always default to the generic unless you ask. When a doctor prescribes something, ask directly: "Is there a generic available for this?" When dropping off at the pharmacy, confirm the generic will be dispensed. Sometimes what gets filled is the brand name by default simply because no one asked otherwise.

## Request a 90-Day Supply Instead of 30

Most pharmacies and mail-order services charge less per pill for a 90-day supply than three separate 30-day fills. If you're on a maintenance medication you take long-term (blood pressure, thyroid, cholesterol, antidepressants), switching to 90-day fills typically saves 10-25% and reduces the number of trips to the pharmacy. Ask your doctor to write the prescription for a 90-day quantity, then confirm your insurance or discount card applies to the larger fill.

## Check If a Manufacturer Coupon Applies

Most brand-name drugs have a manufacturer patient assistance coupon — often found directly on the drug's official website or through NeedyMeds.org. These coupons can bring a $400/month specialty drug down to $10-$35 for people who have commercial insurance. They typically don't work with Medicaid or Medicare, but for anyone with employer or marketplace insurance, it's worth a five-minute search before assuming you're stuck with the full price.

## Ask About the Cash Price

If you don't have insurance or you're in a high-deductible plan that hasn't been met, ask the pharmacy for the straight cash price before any card or coupon. Some pharmacies (particularly warehouse clubs and independent pharmacies) have negotiated cash prices lower than what any third party can offer. The $4 and $10 generic programs at large chains are cash prices that have been available for years.

## Talk to Your Doctor About Therapeutic Alternatives

Two drugs can treat the same condition at very different price points. If what you're prescribed is expensive, ask your doctor: "Is there a generic in the same class that would work for my situation?" This is a legitimate clinical conversation, not an awkward ask — doctors often don't know the out-of-pocket cost of what they're prescribing and genuinely want to help you stay on treatment at a price you can maintain.

## Patient Assistance Programs for Uninsured or Underinsured

Most major pharmaceutical manufacturers run patient assistance programs (PAPs) that provide medications free or at very low cost to people who meet income requirements and don't have adequate insurance coverage. NeedyMeds.org and RxAssist.org are free databases that match you with programs for specific drugs. The application process takes time, but the savings are substantial — often 100% of the cost for qualifying patients.

## The Bottom Line

Prescription costs have more flexibility than most people realize — they're just not transparent about it unless you ask. The fastest wins: look up the GoodRx price before filling anything, confirm the generic is being dispensed, and switch to a 90-day supply for any maintenance medication. Stack those three moves together and most people cut their prescription spending by 30-60% without any change in their treatment.
