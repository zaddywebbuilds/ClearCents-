Reselling is one of the oldest side hustles in the world: buy something for less than it's worth, sell it to someone who wants it, keep the difference. No special skills, no degree, no startup capital beyond a small sourcing budget.

The modern version — hitting thrift stores and clearance racks, then listing on eBay, Facebook Marketplace, Poshmark, or Mercari — has produced thousands of people earning $1,000–$5,000/month part-time. Here's the complete beginner's playbook.

## Why Reselling Works

The core opportunity: most sellers don't know what they have. Thrift stores price by category ("all shirts $3"), not by brand. Estate sales price to liquidate, not to maximize. Facebook Marketplace sellers price for a quick local sale, not to reach the national market that eBay provides.

Your job is to know more than the seller. That knowledge gap is your profit margin.

## Step 1: Choose Your Platform (Start With One)

Each platform has different buyers, fees, and item types. Pick one to start — don't spread across five at once.

**eBay** — largest audience, best for electronics, collectibles, brand-name clothing, vintage items, niche products. Auction format or fixed price. Shipping is required. Fees: ~13% of final sale price. Best reach for unusual items.

**Facebook Marketplace** — local sales, no shipping required, no fees for most sales. Best for furniture, appliances, tools, large items. Buyers pick up in person. Faster cash but smaller audience.

**Poshmark** — clothing, shoes, accessories only. Built-in buyer community. Flat 20% fee on sales over $15. Good for brand-name and designer fashion.

**Mercari** — similar to eBay but simpler interface. Good for electronics, toys, games, clothing. Fees around 10% + payment processing.

**OfferUp** — similar to Facebook Marketplace for local sales. Good for mid-size items.

**Recommendation for beginners:** Start with eBay for most items. The larger buyer pool means faster sales and better prices on hard-to-find items. Use Facebook Marketplace for anything you don't want to ship.

## Step 2: Research Before You Buy (The Only Rule That Matters)

Never buy something to resell without checking what it actually sells for. Not what it's listed for — what it **sold** for.

On eBay: search the item → filter by "Sold listings" (left sidebar). This shows real completed transactions at real prices.

This takes 60 seconds and is the difference between profit and loss. A thrift store Patagonia jacket priced at $12 might sell for $85 on eBay — or it might be a common style that gets $18. Check every time until you develop instincts.

**Price formula:**

`Profit = Sale price − source cost − platform fee (13%) − shipping cost`

If a jacket sells for $60 on eBay:
- Minus 13% fee: $7.80
- Minus shipping (USPS flat rate medium box): ~$16
- Minus source cost: $12
- **Profit: $24.20**

That's 35 minutes of work (photo, list, pack, ship) for $24. Not every item will be this good — some sell for less than expected, some sit for weeks. But the math works across a volume of items.

## Step 3: Where to Source

**Thrift stores** — Goodwill, Salvation Army, Savers, local charity shops. Check the clothing racks for brand-name items. Check electronics for working items (test before buying — most thrift stores allow it). Visit on weekday mornings when new stock comes out.

**Garage sales and estate sales** — Friday and Saturday mornings. Estate sales are gold for tools, vintage items, collectibles, and household goods at pennies on the dollar. Check estatesales.net and Craigslist for listings.

**Retail clearance** — Home Depot, Target, and Walmart clearance sections regularly have items marked 70–90% off that still sell at higher prices on Amazon or eBay. This is called "retail arbitrage." Use the Amazon Seller app to scan barcodes in-store and see current Amazon prices instantly.

**Your own home** — Start here. Go through your closets, garage, and storage. Clothes you haven't worn in a year, electronics you replaced, sports gear gathering dust — list it all. This costs nothing and teaches you the listing process before you spend money on inventory.

**Facebook Marketplace "free" section** — People give away furniture, appliances, and household items they don't want. Many of these sell locally for $50–$200 with no cleaning needed.

## Step 4: Photo and Listing Best Practices

Photos are your storefront. Poor photos = no sale.

**Photo rules:**
- Natural light, never flash
- Clean background (white wall, white sheet, or outdoors)
- Multiple angles: front, back, sides, labels/tags, any defects
- Clothing: photograph flat on a clean surface or on a hanger against white

**Title formula for eBay:**
`Brand + Item type + Key features + Size/Color + Condition`

Example: `Patagonia Men's Better Sweater Fleece Jacket Blue Large EUC`

Use the exact words buyers search for. Include the brand name, size, and relevant style keywords. Check sold listings for successful titles and model yours similarly.

**Condition:** Be accurate. Over-describe flaws rather than under-describe. A buyer who receives something worse than described will leave negative feedback or open a return. One honest "small stain on inner lining, not visible when worn" beats one return.

**Pricing:** Start at or slightly below the average sold price. You can always drop the price — you can't raise it after listing.

## Step 5: Shipping Without Losing Money

Shipping kills beginners more than anything else. Know your costs before you price.

**USPS options:**
- **First Class:** under 16 oz, cheapest option (~$4–6)
- **Priority Mail Flat Rate boxes:** fixed price regardless of weight. Medium box ~$16, Large ~$22. Great for heavy items that fit.
- **Ground Advantage:** replaces Parcel Select for packages 1–70 lbs. Often cheapest for items 1–5 lbs.

**eBay's shipping discounts:** eBay gets commercial rates significantly below retail. Always use eBay's label printing — you'll pay 30–40% less than going to the post office.

**Weigh items before listing.** A kitchen scale is a $10 investment that pays for itself on the first item. Guessing shipping weight wrong is how beginners lose money.

**Offer free shipping only when you've priced it in.** "Free shipping" listings rank higher in eBay search, but the cost comes out of your margin. Build it into the price if you want to offer it.

## What Actually Sells: The Most Profitable Categories

| Category | Where to source | Average margin |
|----------|----------------|----------------|
| Brand-name clothing | Thrift stores | $15–$60/item |
| Vintage clothing (pre-1990) | Thrift, estate sales | $20–$100/item |
| Sneakers (Nike, Jordan, Adidas) | Thrift, clearance | $30–$150/item |
| LEGO sets (complete) | Garage sales, Facebook | $20–$80/set |
| Power tools | Estate sales, Marketplace | $20–$100/item |
| Video games and consoles | Thrift, garage sales | $10–$60/item |
| Vintage kitchenware (cast iron, Pyrex) | Thrift, estate sales | $10–$80/item |

**Avoid until you're experienced:** furniture (shipping is brutal), anything fragile, designer goods without authentication knowledge (fakes are a real risk and eBay can ban your account).

## Building Volume and Systems

One item per week is a hobby. Twenty items per week is a real income stream.

The difference is systems. Successful resellers batch their work:
- **One sourcing day per week** (Saturday morning garage sales + thrift stops)
- **One photo/listing session** (Sunday afternoon — photograph and list everything at once)
- **Daily shipping** (print labels in the morning, drop at the post office or schedule pickup)

A $500/month reselling income typically requires listing 20–40 items per month at $15–$30 average profit. That's 5–10 sourcing hours and 10–15 listing/shipping hours. Realistic for someone doing this on evenings and weekends alongside a full-time job — like what the [start-a-side-hustle-while-working guide](/side-hustles/start-side-hustle-while-working-full-time/) covers in detail.

## What to Do With the Money

Don't spend your reselling income as fast as it comes in. The most successful resellers reinvest a portion into better inventory while keeping some for taxes (set aside 25–30% for the IRS if you're profitable).

Once you're consistently making $300–$500/month, consider whether to scale into a niche you know well — vintage clothing, tools, or electronics — or keep the variety that protects you when one category slows down.
