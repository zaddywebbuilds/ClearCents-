Two people have identical debt: a $10,000 credit card at 22% and a $1,500 store card at 18%. They both commit $500/month above minimums to debt payoff.

Person A (snowball) pays off the $1,500 store card first. Done in under four months. Momentum builds. They redirect the freed-up payment to the credit card.

Person B (avalanche) puts the $500 directly toward the $10,000 credit card — the higher rate. They don't pay off any debt for over a year, but they save hundreds in interest.

Both will eventually be debt-free. The question is which approach gets you there.

## The Math: Avalanche Wins

Let's use real numbers. Suppose you have:

| Debt | Balance | Interest Rate | Minimum Payment |
|------|---------|---------------|-----------------|
| Credit card A | $8,000 | 24% APR | $160 |
| Credit card B | $3,200 | 19% APR | $65 |
| Personal loan | $5,500 | 11% APR | $140 |

**Monthly minimum total: $365**  
**Extra payment per month: $400**  
**Total monthly payment: $765**

**Snowball order:** Personal loan → Credit card B → Credit card A  
**Time to debt-free:** ~28 months | **Total interest paid: ~$4,100**

**Avalanche order:** Credit card A → Credit card B → Personal loan  
**Time to debt-free:** ~26 months | **Total interest paid: ~$3,200**

The avalanche saves roughly **$900 and 2 months**. On larger debts with higher rates, the gap can reach $5,000–$10,000+.

## The Psychology: Snowball Often Wins

The avalanche is optimal on paper. But debt payoff is a long game that requires sustained motivation, and motivation is not mathematical.

Harvard Business Review research found that people with multiple debts were more likely to pay off all their debt when they concentrated on one account at a time and prioritized the smallest balance. The measurable progress of eliminating an entire account — getting it to zero, closing it, crossing it off the list — generates momentum that sustains effort over months and years.

The 24% credit card may be the mathematical priority. But if you're looking at a $8,000 balance that barely moves for the first year while you grind it down, you're more likely to lose motivation and slow or stop extra payments.

The snowball converts long-term progress into short-term wins. For people who have attempted debt payoff and stopped before finishing, this matters more than the interest savings.

## How to Choose

**Choose the avalanche if:**
- Your high-interest debt is also your largest debt (the most common case)
- You have strong intrinsic motivation and don't need early payoff wins to stay on track
- The interest rate gap is large (24% vs 9%) — the savings are substantial enough to justify the slower early progress
- You've successfully stuck to a financial plan for extended periods before

**Choose the snowball if:**
- You have several small debts you can eliminate quickly — the early wins are meaningful
- You've tried debt payoff before and stopped — you need the psychological structure
- Your interest rates are similar across debts (12% vs 16%) — the difference in total interest paid is small
- The motivation to see accounts go to zero is genuinely important to you

**The hybrid approach** (often the right answer): Use the snowball to eliminate 1–2 small debts fast (3–4 months), then switch to avalanche for the remaining high-rate debt. You get the early wins without overpaying interest on large balances for years.

## Setting Up Either Method in 4 Steps

**1. List all debts**  
Write down every debt: balance, interest rate, minimum payment, and lender. If you're not sure of your balances or current interest rates, [Credit Karma](https://creditkarma.com) shows all your accounts, credit score, and debt summary for free — no credit card required.

**2. Choose your order**  
Snowball: sort by balance, smallest to largest.  
Avalanche: sort by interest rate, highest to lowest.

**3. Identify your extra payment**  
This is the amount above minimums you can consistently send each month. Even $100/month matters. Use our [budgeting framework](/budgeting/50-30-20-rule/) to find this number in your income.

**4. Make only minimum payments on all debts except your target**  
Every extra dollar goes to debt #1 on your list. When #1 is gone, roll its minimum payment (plus your extra payment) to debt #2. Repeat.

## What About Balance Transfers and Consolidation?

Both methods assume you're not changing the interest rates on your existing debt. If you can qualify for a **0% balance transfer card** (typically requires 680+ credit score), transferring high-rate credit card balances to a 0% promotional period changes the math dramatically.

At 0% for 18 months, every payment goes entirely to principal. The avalanche becomes irrelevant — everything is equal. Focus on paying the transferred balance before the promotional period ends.

Similarly, a **debt consolidation loan** at 8–10% on balances currently at 20%+ interest fundamentally changes your payoff timeline. See your options before committing to a multi-year payoff of high-rate balances if refinancing is available to you.

## The Number That Matters Most

Neither method works if you don't stay consistent. The most common reason debt payoff stalls is lifestyle creep: as income grows, spending grows proportionally, and the extra payment never materializes.

Automate your extra debt payment on the same day your paycheck lands. Before you spend on wants, the payment is already made. This is the single structural change that makes both methods work.

The debt you don't pay today costs you interest tomorrow. Whether you pick snowball or avalanche, starting now is worth more than picking the perfect method and waiting.
