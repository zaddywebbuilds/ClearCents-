The most common investing mistake isn't picking the wrong stock. It's waiting until you have "enough" money to start.

There is no threshold. The mechanics of investing at $100 are identical to investing at $10,000 — same accounts, same funds, same compound growth. The only difference is scale. And scale comes from starting early and staying consistent, not from waiting until the number feels significant.

Here's exactly how to invest your first $100.

## Before You Invest: One Prerequisite

Investing with high-interest debt is a losing trade. If you're carrying credit card balances at 18–24% APR, paying those down first is a guaranteed 18–24% return — better than the stock market's historical 10% average.

The exception: if your employer offers a 401(k) match, contribute enough to get the full match before paying down debt. A 50% or 100% employer match is an immediate return no investment can compete with.

Once high-interest debt is eliminated (or you have no debt), you're ready to invest.

## Step 1: Open the Right Account First

Where you invest matters as much as what you invest in. The wrong account costs you thousands in unnecessary taxes.

**The Roth IRA** is the right first account for most new investors:

- You contribute money you've already paid taxes on
- Everything inside grows completely tax-free
- Qualified withdrawals in retirement are 100% tax-free
- You can withdraw your original contributions (not earnings) at any time without penalty — it's not locked away forever
- 2025 annual limit: $7,000 ($8,000 if 50+)
- Income limit: $161,000 (single) / $240,000 (married filing jointly)

**Where to open it:** Fidelity and Schwab are the best options for beginners. No account minimums, no trading fees, fractional shares available, and excellent educational resources. Either one is fine.

**If you don't qualify for a Roth IRA** (income too high, or no earned income), open a traditional brokerage account at the same brokerages.

## Step 2: Fund It With $100

After opening the account, link your bank account and transfer $100. This typically takes 1–3 business days to clear. Some brokerages let you start investing immediately while the transfer is pending.

Set up automatic monthly transfers at the same time. Even $50 or $100/month on autopilot builds a habit and takes advantage of dollar-cost averaging — buying more shares when prices are low, fewer when they're high.

## Step 3: Buy One Index Fund

With $100 in your Roth IRA, buy a single broad index fund. Don't overthink this step.

**Best options for beginners:**

| Fund | Type | Expense Ratio | Minimum |
|------|------|---------------|---------|
| FXAIX | S&P 500 (Fidelity) | 0.015% | $0 |
| FZROX | Total Market (Fidelity) | 0.00% | $0 |
| SWTSX | Total Market (Schwab) | 0.03% | $0 |
| VTI | Total Market ETF | 0.03% | ~$1 (fractional) |

These funds hold hundreds to thousands of companies. By buying one fund, you're automatically diversified across most of the US economy.

The expense ratio is the annual fee charged by the fund. At 0.015%, you're paying $0.15 per year on $1,000 invested — essentially free.

**How to buy:** In your brokerage account, search the ticker symbol (FXAIX, VTI, etc.), click "Buy," choose "dollar amount" instead of "shares" if fractional shares are available, and enter $100. Review the order and submit.

You now own a fractional share of hundreds of American companies.

## What Happens Next: Compound Growth

Your $100 doesn't sit still. It buys ownership stakes in companies that generate revenue, pay dividends, and grow in value. Over time, that growth compounds — you earn returns on your returns.

**The math on $100/month:**

| Years | Total Invested | Value at 7% return |
|-------|---------------|-------------------|
| 10 | $12,000 | ~$17,000 |
| 20 | $24,000 | ~$52,000 |
| 30 | $36,000 | ~$113,000 |
| 40 | $48,000 | ~$240,000 |

The money you didn't invest grows nothing. The money you invested is working every day, including nights, weekends, and market holidays.

## Common Beginner Mistakes to Avoid

**Checking daily.** Markets move up and down constantly. Looking at your balance every day trains you to react emotionally to normal volatility. Check monthly or quarterly.

**Selling when the market drops.** Every significant market drop in history has recovered and gone on to new highs. Selling during a downturn locks in losses and means you miss the recovery. The correct action during a market drop is to keep investing (or invest more).

**Buying individual stocks first.** Individual companies can go to zero. Diversified index funds cannot — they automatically replace failing companies. Learn how markets work through index funds before picking individual stocks.

**Waiting for the "right time."** Time in the market beats timing the market. The best time to invest was 10 years ago. The second best time is now.

**Ignoring fees.** An expense ratio of 1% sounds small but costs you significantly over time. A 1% fee on a $100,000 portfolio is $1,000/year — money that compounds against you. Stick with funds under 0.10%.

## What to Do After Your First $100

1. **Automate monthly contributions** — set up a recurring transfer so investing happens without a decision
2. **Increase contributions over time** — every raise is an opportunity to raise your investment amount before lifestyle inflation absorbs it
3. **Max your Roth IRA** — work toward contributing the full $7,000/year ($583/month) over time
4. **Learn the basics of your 401(k)** — once your Roth IRA is on autopilot, optimize your employer-sponsored plan
5. **Don't complicate it** — one or two index funds is genuinely all most people need for their entire investing life
