# What Is a Debt-to-Income Ratio (and Why It Matters)

Your credit score gets all the attention, but there's a second number quietly deciding whether you get approved for a mortgage, a car loan, or a better interest rate: your debt-to-income ratio, or DTI. It doesn't show up on your credit report, and most people have never calculated their own — right up until a lender does it for them and the answer isn't what they hoped.

## What Debt-to-Income Ratio Actually Means

Your DTI is the percentage of your gross (pre-tax) monthly income that goes toward debt payments. It answers a simple question lenders care about a lot: after your existing debt obligations, how much of your income is actually free to take on a new payment? A high DTI signals you're already stretched, even if your credit score looks great.

## How to Calculate Your Debt-to-Income Ratio

The formula is straightforward:

**DTI = (Total Monthly Debt Payments ÷ Gross Monthly Income) × 100**

**What counts as debt payments:**
- Rent or mortgage payment
- Minimum credit card payments
- Auto loan payments
- Student loan payments
- Personal loan payments
- Any other required monthly debt payment (child support, alimony)

**What doesn't count:**
- Utilities, groceries, insurance premiums (unless bundled into a mortgage)
- Subscriptions and streaming services
- Retirement contributions
- Taxes withheld from your paycheck

**Example:** If your monthly debt payments total $1,800 and your gross monthly income is $5,500, your DTI is ($1,800 ÷ $5,500) × 100 = 32.7%.

## Why It Matters More Than You'd Think

DTI isn't just a mortgage-application formality. It directly affects:

1. **Mortgage approval and rate** — most lenders cap DTI at 43% for a qualified mortgage, and a lower ratio often unlocks a better interest rate even at the same credit score
2. **Auto loan terms** — a high DTI can mean a higher rate or a required larger down payment
3. **Credit limit increases** — issuers frequently pull income-to-debt signals before approving a higher limit
4. **Your own financial cushion** — even outside of any loan application, a high DTI means less room to absorb a job loss, medical bill, or rate increase

## What Counts as a Good Ratio

| DTI Range | What It Signals |
|---|---|
| Below 20% | Excellent — strong approval odds and best rates |
| 20-36% | Healthy — most lenders comfortable |
| 36-43% | Getting tight — approval possible but terms may suffer |
| Above 43% | High risk — many conventional lenders decline |

## How to Lower Your DTI

**Pay off one balance completely, not several partially.** Lenders count minimum payments, not balances. Paying $500 each toward three different credit cards leaves all three minimum payments intact on your DTI. Paying $1,500 toward one card until it hits zero removes that entire payment from the calculation. This is the core logic behind the debt snowball method — see our full comparison of the [debt snowball vs. debt avalanche](/debt-free/debt-snowball-vs-avalanche/) approaches for which fits your situation.

**Attack high-interest debt first if a card is dragging your ratio.** Credit card minimums are often disproportionately high relative to the balance because of the interest baked in. Our guide to [paying off credit card debt](/debt-free/how-to-pay-off-credit-card-debt/) covers the fastest ways to bring that balance — and its minimum payment — down.

**Call and ask for a lower rate or a modified payment.** Sometimes the payment itself, not just the balance, can shrink. Our breakdown of [negotiating with creditors](/debt-free/how-to-negotiate-with-creditors/) covers scripts that work for lowering both.

**Increase income without increasing debt.** A raise, a side hustle, or freelance income all lower DTI by growing the denominator instead of shrinking the numerator — and unlike debt payoff, there's no ceiling on how much this can move the number.

**Avoid new debt in the 3-6 months before a major application.** Even a new $300/month car payment can be enough to push a borderline DTI over a lender's cutoff. If a mortgage or major loan is on the horizon, hold off on financing anything else until after it closes.

## Give It a Real Line in Your Budget

Lowering DTI isn't a one-time calculation — it's an ongoing target your monthly budget should work toward. [Zero-based budgeting](/budgeting/zero-based-budgeting-guide/), where every dollar of income gets assigned a job before the month starts, makes it easy to see exactly how much extra can go toward debt principal each month instead of disappearing into unplanned spending.

## The Bottom Line

Your debt-to-income ratio is one of the few numbers that quietly gatekeeps almost every major financial milestone — a house, a car, a better rate. Unlike your credit score, it moves fast: pay off the right balance, and it can shift within a single statement cycle. Calculate yours today, know where you stand against the 36% benchmark, and target the single payment that will move the needle the most.
