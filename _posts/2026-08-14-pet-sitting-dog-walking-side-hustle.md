Pet sitting and dog walking used to be something you did for a neighbor as a favor, maybe for a little cash under the table. Apps like Rover and Wag turned it into a structured, bookable side income, and demand hasn't slowed — pet ownership climbed steadily over the past several years, and pet owners increasingly pay for care they used to ask a friend to handle for free.

## What It Actually Pays

Rates vary by city and service type, but general ranges look like this:

| Service | Typical rate |
|---|---|
| 20–30 minute walk | $15–25 |
| Drop-in visit (feeding, quick check) | $15–30 |
| Doggy day care (full day) | $25–45 |
| Overnight house sitting | $30–75+ |
| Boarding (pet stays at your home) | $25–60/night |

A sitter doing even a modest 4–6 bookings a week can realistically add $400–800 a month, and sitters who build a strong review base and take overnight and boarding jobs — which pay significantly more per booking than walks — can push well past $1,000 a month working part-time around a regular job.

## Step 1: Decide Which Services You Actually Want to Offer

Not every service suits every schedule. Dog walking fits around a 9-to-5 if you can do midday walks during lunch. Overnight boarding or house sitting works better for people with flexible evenings or weekends, or who don't mind an animal in their home for a few days. Pick 1–2 services that fit your actual availability rather than signing up for everything and getting overwhelmed by scheduling conflicts.

## Step 2: Choose Your Platform (or Go Independent)

Rover and Wag are the two dominant platforms in the US. Rover tends to pay more per booking and covers a wider range of services; Wag leans toward quick, on-demand walks. Both handle payment processing, provide liability coverage for platform bookings, and — critically for a new sitter — bring you clients you'd otherwise have no way to find. The tradeoff is their commission, usually 15–20% of each booking. Once you've built a base of repeat clients, some sitters transition part of their business to direct bookings to avoid the platform cut, but starting on a platform is almost always the faster path to your first paying clients.

## Step 3: Build a Profile That Actually Gets Booked

Pet owners are trusting you with a family member — your profile needs to earn that trust fast. A profile with a clear photo of you (ideally with a dog), a specific bio mentioning any relevant experience (even just "grew up with two labs" counts), and your exact availability converts far better than a vague one. Ask friends or family with pets to book your very first few jobs if needed, purely to generate initial reviews — a profile with zero reviews is a hard sell to a stranger.

## Step 4: Price to Get Your First Bookings, Then Raise Rates

New sitters often do better pricing slightly below the local average for the first month or two to build a review base quickly, then raising rates once they have 5–10 five-star reviews. Reviews matter more than price to most pet owners choosing between sitters, so the fastest path to higher pay is a strong review history, not the lowest price on the platform.

## What It Costs to Get Started

This is a genuinely low-overhead side hustle. There's no license requirement in most areas, and startup costs are limited to basics: a sturdy leash, waste bags, maybe a treat pouch. The one cost worth taking seriously is liability insurance — bookings made through Rover or Wag typically include coverage automatically, which is one more reason to start on a platform rather than fully independent.
